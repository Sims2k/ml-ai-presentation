\begin{saybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item A few ML terms connect familiar application data to a learned risk estimate.
\item \textbf{Feature}: a prepared input, such as logins during the previous seven days.
\item \textbf{Label}: the observed churn outcome used for training and later evaluation.
\item \textbf{Prediction}: an estimate produced before the customer’s future outcome is known.
\item \textbf{Training vs. inference}: learn from earlier examples; apply the approved model to current inputs.
\item Ana / C042: logins fall from 7 to 1; the illustrative score is 0.70. Her next-30-day outcome is unknown.
\item \textit{Lower activity is evidence for a model input, not proof that a customer will leave.}
\end{itemize}
\end{saybox}
\begin{examplebox}
Let's meet the example that will follow us through the whole talk. Ana is a fictional customer, customer number C042. Over the last week, her app logins dropped from seven down to just one — that's a feature, a piece of prepared input data. Based on that and other signals, our model estimates her churn risk at 0.70. That's a prediction — a guess made before we know what actually happens. Only later, once we observe whether she actually leaves, do we get the label. It's really important to say clearly: a lower login count is evidence, not proof. Ana hasn't left the bank — not yet, maybe not ever.
\end{examplebox}

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{saybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item Architecture connects learning, production data, operational controls, and business decisions.
\item \textbf{Training} uses historical features and labels to build and evaluate candidates.
\item \textbf{Production} validates current data and applies an approved, compatible model.
\item \textbf{Stored scores} link predictions to customer, time, input evidence, and versions.
\item \textbf{Business policy} determines the permitted action; it remains separate from the score.
\item \textit{At our bank,} the bank computes a monthly batch of churn scores, then applies campaign eligibility and contact rules.
\item \textit{Keep the learning path and production path distinct, but traceable.}
\end{itemize}
\end{saybox}
\begin{examplebox}
So how does a model like the one that scored Ana actually become part of a real system? There's a training side, where we learn from historical data, and a production side, where we apply an already-approved model to current customers. Every score we produce gets stored together with who it was for, when it happened, and which model version made it. And crucially, the business policy — who actually gets contacted — sits completely separate from that score. At our bank, that means the churn score is computed once a month, in a batch, and only afterward does the campaign team apply its own eligibility rules.
\end{examplebox}

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{saybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item Code correctness and predictive usefulness depend on different feedback and release cycles.
\item \textbf{Software changes} can be checked through code, contract, and integration tests.
\item \textbf{Model development} also needs representative data, suitable labels, and evaluation.
\item \textbf{Feedback differs}: execution checks may be immediate; churn labels arrive later.
\item \textbf{Co-versioning} links changes across code, feature meaning, model, and configuration.
\item \textit{At our bank,} a CRM connector can change today while M1 stays fixed; its changed inputs can still alter predictions.
\item \textit{A passing software test does not establish future predictive quality.}
\end{itemize}
\end{saybox}
\begin{examplebox}
Here's something that trips a lot of teams up: code and models don't change on the same clock. You can test a code change quickly — does it compile, does it pass its tests. But testing whether a model is still predictively useful takes much longer, because you need real labels, and those arrive late. Imagine our bank's CRM connector changes today. The code deploys fine, the tests pass — but the model, M1, hasn't changed at all. Yet its inputs have, and that alone can shift what it predicts. So a green test suite is not a guarantee that your predictions are still good.
\end{examplebox}

\end{itemize}
\end{cuebox}
\end{minipage}
\vspace{2mm}
\begin{saybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item A model estimates risk. Business rules decide whether and how the bank may act.
\item \textbf{Score}: estimated churn risk, not a guaranteed outcome.
\item \textbf{Policy}: preferences, eligibility, contact limits, and campaign budget.
\item \textbf{Action}: an offer, another response, or no contact at all.
\item \textbf{Separate evaluation}: predictive quality and the benefit of an offer are different questions.
\item Ana may have a high risk score and still receive no offer because contact preferences or policy prohibit it.
\item \textit{A policy change should not require retraining the prediction model.}
\end{itemize}
\end{saybox}
\begin{examplebox}
A model's score is just an estimate — never a guaranteed outcome. What happens next is governed by business policy: contact preferences, who's eligible, how many times someone can be contacted, and the campaign's budget. So picture Ana again. Even with a high churn-risk score, she might receive no offer at all, simply because policy says no. That separation matters a lot in practice — it means if the bank wants to change its contact rules tomorrow, nobody needs to retrain the model to do it.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{saybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item Select the interaction pattern from the decision’s timing and failure behavior.
\item \textbf{Batch}: compute many predictions; consumers read stored results later.
\item \textbf{Synchronous}: the caller waits for a result during its request.
\item \textbf{Asynchronous}: an event producer and consumer are decoupled through messaging.
\item \textbf{Edge}: a deployment location, independent of prediction timing.
\item \textit{At our bank,} the churn campaign can use precomputed scores. An immediate fraud check motivates a different timing need.
\item \textit{Timing, location, and release strategy are separate choices.}
\end{itemize}
\end{saybox}
\begin{examplebox}
Now Tomás is going to walk us through how predictions actually get delivered. The first question to ask is timing: does someone need this answer right now, or can it wait? For our churn case, a batch works fine — scores get computed overnight and read later by the campaign. But imagine a fraud check instead: that needs an answer synchronously, while the customer is still mid-transaction. And there's a third option, asynchronous, where producer and consumer are decoupled through messaging. Notice that where the model physically runs — centrally or at the edge — is a completely separate decision from any of this.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{saybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item A complete validated batch must be available before its business deadline.
\item \textbf{Snapshot}: define population, cutoff, and required source freshness.
\item \textbf{Validate}: reject incompatible inputs before scoring.
\item \textbf{Reconcile}: account for written, rejected, and withheld records.
\item \textbf{Publish}: expose a validated batch under a defined consumer contract.
\item \textit{At our bank,} monthly churn scores are saved for the campaign. A real numerical deadline must be agreed with campaign owners.
\item \textit{Optimize the whole batch deadline, not only per-record prediction speed.}
\end{itemize}
\end{saybox}
\begin{examplebox}
Batch serving means computing lots of predictions at once, ahead of when they're needed. For the bank, that means defining a clear snapshot: which customers, as of when, using which sources. Before scoring anyone, we validate the inputs and reject anything that doesn't meet the contract. We reconcile what got written, what got rejected, and what had to be withheld. Only then do we publish — expose a complete, validated batch under a contract the campaign team can rely on. The real goal isn't making any single prediction fast; it's hitting the deadline for the whole batch.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{saybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item Prediction becomes part of the application’s response path.
\item \textbf{Response time} includes lookup, preparation, inference, and network work.
\item \textbf{Availability} now affects the waiting application’s request.
\item \textbf{Failure behavior} needs explicit timeout, error, and fallback policies.

\begin{examplebox}
Synchronous serving puts the model directly on the critical path of a user's request — they're waiting right now for an answer. That means your response time now has to include looking things up, preparing features, running inference, and the network round-trip. And since the caller is blocked, you need explicit policies for what happens on a timeout or an error. A real-time fraud check is a good candidate for this pattern. Our monthly churn campaign is not — there's no customer sitting there waiting for that score.
\end{examplebox}

\end{itemize}

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{saybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item Messages separate production, processing, and consumption of a prediction.
\item \textbf{Buffering} absorbs bursts and supports independent processing.
\item \textbf{Lag} and queue health become operational concerns.
\item \textbf{Retries} require failure handling and idempotent processing.
\item \textbf{Contracts} define message identity, timing, schema, and result interpretation.
\item \textit{At our bank,} if a future campaign consumes prediction events, a repeated message must not cause duplicate customer contacts.
\item \textit{Decoupling adds messaging responsibilities; it does not remove timing requirements.}
\end{itemize}
\end{saybox}
\begin{examplebox}
Asynchronous serving uses messages to separate who produces a prediction from who eventually consumes it. That buffering is useful — it smooths out bursts of work. But it brings its own concerns: how far behind is the queue, and what happens if a message gets retried? If our bank ever built a campaign that reacted to prediction events in real time, a retried message must never cause the same customer to be contacted twice. Decoupling doesn't remove your timing obligations — it just moves them into a messaging contract.
\end{examplebox}
