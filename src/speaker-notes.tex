\item The code deploys fine, the tests pass — but the current approved model, which we call M1, hasn't changed at all. Yet its inputs have, and that alone can shift what it predicts.
\item So a green test suite is not a guarantee that your predictions are still good.
\end{itemize}
\end{examplebox}

\item \textbf{Label}: the observed churn outcome used for training and later evaluation.
\item \textbf{Prediction}: an estimate produced before the customer’s future outcome is known.

\item That's a prediction — a guess made before we know what actually happens. Only later, once we observe whether she actually leaves, do we get the label.
\item It's really important to say clearly: a lower login count is evidence, not proof. Ana hasn't left the bank — not yet, maybe not ever.
\end{itemize}

\item Handoff cue: this is the brief foundation; Simon revisits the monitoring timeline and delayed labels later.
\end{itemize}
\end{cuebox}
\end{minipage}
\vspace{2mm}
\begin{saybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item A software test checks the run today; only mature labels can check the prediction.
\item \textbf{Today}: tests show the job runs, inputs are valid, and scores are saved.
\item \textbf{Not today}: whether Ana’s 0.70 is right. Her 30-day outcome is unknown.
\item \textbf{Later}: link labels to the scores, then measure precision and recall.
\item \textbf{Fixed model}: same inputs, same score. The uncertainty is predictive.
\item Ana / C042: scored 0.70 today. Only churn labels, available after the 30-day window, show whether she left.
\item \textit{The bank need not wait 30 days to catch an incompatible input.}
\end{itemize}
\end{saybox}
\begin{examplebox}[title after break={SPOKEN SCRIPT --- slide 5 continued}]
\begin{itemize}
\item On scoring day, which we call D0, software tests can tell us that the job finished, the inputs matched their contract, and the scores were saved for the expected customers. These are useful checks we can perform immediately.
\item But Ana's score of 0.70 is a risk estimate, not a statement that she definitely leaves. Her next-30-day outcome does not exist yet. Once the window closes and the labels arrive, we link them to the original scores and evaluate precision and recall across the cohort; one customer's outcome cannot validate a probability estimate on its own.
\item With the same inputs and a fixed model, the score can remain reproducible while the prediction is still uncertain. We do not have to wait for labels to catch a broken input contract. Simon will return to this distinction when he explains what monitoring can check now and what must wait.
\end{itemize}
\end{examplebox}

\begin{saybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item A model estimates risk. Business rules decide whether and how the bank may act.
\item \textbf{Score}: estimated churn risk, not a guaranteed outcome.
\item \textbf{Policy}: preferences, eligibility, contact limits, and campaign budget.
\item \textbf{Action}: an offer, another response, or no contact at all.
\item \textbf{Separate evaluation}: predictive quality and the benefit of an offer are different questions.

\item \textit{A policy change should not require retraining the prediction model.}
\end{itemize}
\end{saybox}
\begin{examplebox}[title after break={SPOKEN SCRIPT --- slide 7 continued}]
\begin{itemize}
\item A model's score is just an estimate — never a guaranteed outcome. What happens next is governed by business policy: contact preferences, who's eligible, how many times someone can be contacted, and the campaign's budget.
\item So picture Ana again. Even with a high churn-risk score, she might receive no offer at all, simply because policy says no.
\item That separation matters a lot in practice — it means if the bank wants to change its contact rules tomorrow, nobody needs to retrain the model to do it.
\end{itemize}

\item Diagram cue: follow Card payment → Fraud API → Authorizer.
\end{itemize}
\end{cuebox}
\end{minipage}
\vspace{2mm}
\begin{saybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item Same domain, different timing needs. A comparison, not a new part of the churn system.
\item \textbf{Model time}: smaller models, leaner code, GPU/TPU at a cost.
\item \textbf{System time}: fast input lookup; precomputed, cached scores.
\item \textbf{Inputs}: the back-end usually fetches features the caller lacks.
\item \textbf{Async}: idempotent consumers, lag and failed-message handling.
\item \textit{At our bank,} fraud blocking is synchronous, fraud alerts asynchronous. Churn stays batch; CRM can read Ana's cached score.
\item \textit{A fast model does not guarantee a fast end-to-end service.}
\end{itemize}
\end{saybox}
\begin{examplebox}[title after break={SPOKEN SCRIPT --- slide 10 continued}]
\begin{itemize}
\item These three rows are comparisons, not extra services we are adding to the churn system. For payment blocking, the authorization service waits for the fraud API's response. That is synchronous. For a fraud alert, the transaction can instead be published as an event, scored by a stream processor, and sent to another application asynchronously.
\item The third row shows a hybrid: churn scores are still computed in batch, but a CRM screen can look up Ana's stored score quickly from a customer-keyed store. A fast lookup is not the same thing as calculating a new prediction during the request.
\item End-to-end response time includes feature lookup and data preparation, not just the model. Smaller models, leaner execution, or accelerators may reduce computation, with cost and quality trade-offs. Asynchronous paths also need idempotent consumers, lag monitoring, and failed-message handling. The bank's monthly churn campaign continues to use batch inference.
\end{itemize}
\end{examplebox}

\item \textbf{PSI:} compares the share of customers in fixed bins. It is useful for seeing a changed input mix, but the result depends on the bins and the reference we chose.
\item \textbf{KS:} compares two continuous distributions and looks for their largest cumulative separation. Sample size affects how readily a difference is detected. The usual continuous-distribution test needs care with discrete counts such as app logins or many tied values; a small p-value is not a measure of business harm.
\item \textbf{KL divergence:} describes a directional difference between distributions, so reversing reference and current changes the interpretation. Categories with zero reference probability need careful handling. \textbf{JS divergence} gives a symmetric comparison, but it still measures distribution difference, not model accuracy.
\item \textbf{Reference} means the named baseline population and period, using the same feature definition. A \textbf{threshold} is the alerting rule we choose for the application, not a universal cutoff. \textbf{Segments} are customer subgroups we check separately, because an overall average can hide a local change. \textbf{Action} means investigate data validity, source changes, and any available outcome evidence; a drift statistic alone does not authorize retraining.
\end{itemize}
\end{examplebox}
